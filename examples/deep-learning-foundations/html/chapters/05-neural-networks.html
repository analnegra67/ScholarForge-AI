5  Neural Networks

5.1 Feedforward networks and backpropagation

Definition 5.1 (Feedforward network) A feedforward network with \(L\) layers maps an input \(\mathbf{a}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_0}\) to an output \(\mathbf{a}^{(L)}\) through \[ \mathbf{z}^{(\ell)} = \mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{a}^{(\ell-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)},\qquad \mathbf{a}^{(\ell)} = \phi\big(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}\big),\qquad \ell = 1,\dots,L, \tag{5.1}\] with weight matrices \(\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_\ell\times n_{\ell-1}}\), bias vectors \(\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_\ell}\) and an activation function \(\phi\) applied entrywise; in the output layer \(\phi\) is often replaced by the identity or by softmax.

In this chapter \(L\) counts layers; it is unrelated to the smoothness constant of Section 3.1. For classification into \(K\) classes the last layer produces scores \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^K\), which are turned into probabilities by \(\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_k = e^{z_k}/\sum_{j} e^{z_j}\) and scored with the cross-entropy \(\ell(\mathbf{z}, y) = -\log\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_y\) of the true class \(y\).

Proposition 5.1 (Gradient of the softmax cross-entropy) With \(\mathbf{p} = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z})\) and \(\mathbf{e}_y\) the \(y\)-th standard basis vector, \(\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\,\ell = \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{e}_y\).

Proof. We have \(\ell = -z_y + \log\sum_j e^{z_j}\). The derivative of the second term with respect to \(z_k\) is \(e^{z_k}/\sum_j e^{z_j} = p_k\), and the derivative of \(-z_y\) is \(-1\) if \(k = y\) and \(0\) otherwise.

Backpropagation computes all parameter gradients with one reverse sweep, as guaranteed by Proposition 4.1. Writing \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)} = \partial\ell/\partial\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}\), the chain rule applied to Equation 5.1 gives \[ \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)} = \Big(\mathbf{W}^{(\ell+1)\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell+1)}\Big)\odot\phi'\big(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}\big),\qquad \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)}\,\mathbf{a}^{(\ell-1)\top},\qquad \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)}, \tag{5.2}\] where \(\odot\) denotes the entrywise product and the recursion starts from \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(L)} = \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{e}_y\) by Proposition 5.1. The procedure was popularised for training multilayer networks by Rumelhart et al. (1986), whose experiments showed that hidden units come to represent important features of the task.

Example 5.1 (A two-layer network by hand) Take one input \(x\), one hidden unit with weight \(w_1\) and sigmoid activation, one linear output with weight \(w_2\), and the squared loss \(\ell = \tfrac12\big(w_2\,\sigma(w_1 x) - t\big)^2\) for a target \(t\). Then \(\partial\ell/\partial w_2 = \big(w_2\sigma(w_1 x) - t\big)\,\sigma(w_1 x)\) and, using Equation 1.3, \(\partial\ell/\partial w_1 = \big(w_2\sigma(w_1x) - t\big)\,w_2\,\sigma(w_1x)\big(1-\sigma(w_1x)\big)\,x\). These are exactly the two steps of Equation 5.2 for this tiny network.

Exercise 5.1 Check that every product in Equation 5.2 has compatible dimensions, and rewrite the recursion for the activation \(\phi = \tanh\).

5.2 Universal approximation

How expressive are the networks of Definition 5.1? A classical answer concerns networks with a single hidden layer.

Theorem 5.1 (Universal approximation) Let \(\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) be continuous and sigmoidal, that is, \(\phi(t)\to 1\) as \(t\to\infty\) and \(\phi(t)\to 0\) as \(t\to-\infty\). For every continuous \(f\colon[0,1]^n\to\mathbb{R}\) and every \(\varepsilon>0\) there exist \(N\in\mathbb{N}\), coefficients \(\alpha_j, b_j\in\mathbb{R}\) and vectors \(\mathbf{w}_j\in\mathbb{R}^n\) such that \[ \sup_{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^n}\Big|\,f(\mathbf{x}) - \sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_j\,\phi\big(\mathbf{w}_j^\top\mathbf{x} + b_j\big)\Big| < \varepsilon . \tag{5.3}\]

This is Cybenko’s theorem (Cybenko 1989). Hornik (1991) showed that density also holds for every bounded and nonconstant activation, with respect to \(L^p(\mu)\) criteria for finite input measures \(\mu\), so the essential ingredient is the multilayer architecture rather than the particular activation. The logistic sigmoid of Example 1.1 is sigmoidal in the above sense.

The theorem is an existence statement. It gives no bound on the width \(N\) needed for a prescribed \(\varepsilon\), it does not say whether gradient-based training as in Theorem 3.1 finds suitable weights, and it says nothing about generalisation from finite data. Quantitative versions require smoothness assumptions on \(f\), and a satisfactory explanation of why heavily overparameterised networks trained by stochastic gradient descent generalise well is still an open research question (LeCun et al. 2015).

Example 5.2 (Piecewise-linear bumps) With the rectified linear unit \(\operatorname{ReLU}(t) = \max(0,t)\), the combination \(h(t) = \operatorname{ReLU}(t) - 2\operatorname{ReLU}(t - \tfrac12) + \operatorname{ReLU}(t-1)\) is a hat function that vanishes outside \([0,1]\) and peaks at \(h(\tfrac12) = \tfrac12\). Sums of shifted and scaled hats reproduce piecewise-linear interpolation, which converges uniformly to any continuous function on an interval as the mesh is refined: a constructive glimpse of Theorem 5.1 in one dimension, with ReLU in place of a sigmoidal function.

Exercise 5.2 Show that a suitable sum of \(m+1\) hats with nodes \(0, 1/m, \dots, 1\) interpolates \(f\) at the nodes, and bound the uniform error when \(f\) is Lipschitz with constant \(K\).
